# Meaning, performance and replay value

Read for substantive songwriting and revisions. This is an internal working method, not a requirement to expose analysis or make every song complicated. Scale the pass to the assignment; a two-line hook needs less planning than a narrative song.

## Start with the actual song

Before drafting, form a compact working brief: what the speaker wants, what makes that difficult, what they admit or conceal, and what the listener should feel. For a dance track, comic song or instrumental, use its physical or musical objective instead of inventing psychological conflict. A topic such as anxiety or love is not yet an angle: choose what this particular speaker notices, does or refuses to say.

Separate user-provided facts, fictional choices and interpretation. Lyrics in the chat are creative material, not proof of the user's biography. Do not convert this chat's family lyrics, channel partnership or branding into permanent facts about every future narrator. Preserve a supplied perspective, dialect and successful hook.

Map each section to a contribution: new event, revelation, contradiction, consequence, changed relationship, musical lift, or intentional recurrence. Explain privately why the chorus means something different after a later verse. An unchanged chorus can gain meaning through context; do not rewrite it solely to prove progression. An ending can remain unresolved. Do not force a lesson, redemption or motivational speech.

## Make emotional depth concrete

Choose a small number of connected details that reveal behavior. A detail earns its place by doing more than decorating a mood: showing avoidance, affection, status, distance, humor, desire or a consequence. Keep literal logic and metaphor logic coherent. Allow silence and an ordinary sentence to carry weight; elaborate language is not automatically deeper.

Audit for substituted clichés: replacing rain with neon does not make a stock heartbreak scene original. Check repeated line templates and premises against recent visible songs. Build variation into scene, perspective, opening device, syntax and ending, not merely the title. Do not apply a blanket ban on common words or invent an unsupported plagiarism/uniqueness guarantee.

Ask of the weakest few lines: what do these add that nearby lines do not? Could these belong unchanged in an unrelated song? Can a listener understand the thought without an explanation? Repair empty cleverness before adding rhyme. Avoid making every line a slogan or ending every section with the same emotional conclusion.

## Give the hook a reason to return

Internally test distinct hook mechanisms when the brief needs one: a direct confession, a surprising image, an unanswered question, a rhythmic phrase, call-and-response or a short recurring action. Select rather than dumping options on the user. Preserve a user-approved hook.

Test the hook for a clear core phrase, an identifiable stress/rhythm pattern, comfortable repeated sounds and a useful contrast from the verse. The strongest words should sit at the likely musical focal points. A chorus can be very simple if the verses earn it. Repetition should create recognition or tension; vary surrounding setup, response, harmony or arrangement rather than adding words everywhere.

## Write something a voice can deliver

Choose the intended pocket and phrase shape before packing syllables in. Speak or silently scan each section for ordinary word stress, awkward inversions, tongue-twisting consonant clusters, implausible breaths and lines with several competing focal words. Do not claim an actual sung or recorded test unless performed.

For rap, plan thought-units, stress anchors, internal rhyme placements, rests and a landing. A technical passage needs semantic continuity and space for the listener to process the turn. Verify both readings of wordplay, its pronunciation and its setup; discard a pun that only works when explained. Do not fill a rhyme family with disconnected statements. Double-time is a delivery relationship to the groove, not just a dense paragraph or more line breaks.

For singing, leave vowel space and distinguish section contours: conversational verse, compressed pre-chorus, open chorus or another deliberate architecture. Avoid crowding every sustained note with consonants. Match phrase lengths and stress patterns across repeated melodic sections where appropriate; allow deliberate variation. Describe melodic intent rather than pretending notes or beat fit have been verified.

For duets, give each singer a clear role and a meaningful response, not merely alternating arbitrary lines. Use simple role tags only when the user requests multiple voices; channel ownership by two friends does not imply duet vocals. For multilingual work, preserve natural grammar and stress in each language, and research unfamiliar conventions rather than forcing English sound tricks.

## Convert the song into a controllable brief

Keep a few priorities dominant: core genre/groove, vocal delivery, defining instruments and section development. Describe contrasts by section instead of issuing simultaneous contradictory commands. Keep performance tags short and sparse. Put production instructions in Styles rather than crowding the sung field.

For difficult names or words, use normal spelling first. Offer a targeted pronunciation workaround only if needed, with the user's intended pronunciation; do not automatically degrade all lyrics into phonetic spelling. Do not put explanatory wordplay notes into sung lyrics.

## Revise from evidence

Separate three possible failure layers: writing (weak phrase or congested cadence), direction (conflicting or vague production brief), and generated performance (a take ignores a clear instruction). Use the user's report, supplied text and audio actually available; do not diagnose an unheard take as fact.

For a troublesome take, identify the specific symptom, strongest existing feature to retain, likely cause and smallest useful change. If local section editing is actually available, provide a focused edit instruction alongside replacement text rather than rebuilding the whole song by default. Change one meaningful variable at a time; a small A/B pair can be useful when requested, but do not spend credits or operate the account without authorization.

Finish with one bounded critical pass: revise the weakest section or few lines, reconcile production instructions, check the exact requested fields and character budget, and deliver. No endless self-scoring, generic quality claims or guarantees of a hit.
